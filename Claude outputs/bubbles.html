Speech & thought — five treatments

All drawn at the real size, on the map's own parchment, in the app's type. What ships today is E, at the bottom, for comparison.

Why the current one reads cheap — three diagnoses, not one:
  • The finish belongs to another app. Flat pastel fills and a grey offset shadow are web-UI grammar. Everything else on this map is drawn — engraved icons, hairline pills, ink on parchment. A filled shape with a shadow is the only object on the canvas that isn't.
  • Colour is doing the work that shape and type should do. Two big blocks of tinted fill shout before the words are read, and the words are the point.
  • Placement is arithmetic, not composition. They sit at a fixed offset from the node's origin, so they collide with the label, the link chips and each other, and they never respond to what is actually around the figure.

A · Engraved plate

RecommendedEngraved plate

The app's own card, shrunk. Parchment ground, one hairline, no fill and no shadow. Speech carries a solid rule and a drawn tail; thought carries a dashed rule and no tail at all — it fades out through three diminishing marks. The two are told apart by line quality and type, the way an engraving tells a solid object from a remembered one.

Why it works hereIt is the same object as every dialog, pill and card in the app, at a smaller size — so it reads as Dystoria rather than as a feature bolted on. Nothing competes with the words.
The costLess instantly "comic" than a cloud. The dashed rule has to be heavy enough to read at 60% zoom — worth checking on a real map before committing.

B · Marginalia

QuietestMarginalia

No container at all. The line is set on the parchment beside the figure, like a note in a margin, tied to the head by a fine leader — solid for speech, dotted for thought. The most confident answer, and the only one that can never collide with anything, because it has no ground to defend.

Why it works hereType alone carries the difference — roman in quotes against grey italic — which is exactly how the rest of the app distinguishes a fact from a reading. Weightless on a crowded map.
The costUnboxed text competes with element labels on a busy scene, and there is no obvious surface to click to edit. Would need a hover ground.

C · Banderole

Most characterfulBanderole

The medieval speech ribbon — how spoken words were drawn for four hundred years before the speech balloon existed, and a close relative of the woodcut icons and heraldic pills already on this map. Speech is a ribbon with folded ends; thought is the same ribbon in a lighter ink, drifting and unpinned.

Why it works hereIt belongs to the same century as the rest of the art, and the curve gives the map something no other element has, so a line of dialogue is never mistaken for a label.
The costThe curved ground makes text-fitting harder — a long line either overflows the ribbon or forces it wide. Best with a firm character cap.

D · One card, both voices

Solves placementOne card, both voices

Not two objects but one, standing directly above the head: the spoken line, a hairline, then the thought beneath it in italic. Always centred, always the same anchor, nothing to collide with — and it puts the contradiction literally one line above the other, which is the whole reason the feature exists.

Why it works hereOne anchor instead of two kills the entire placement problem, and the pair is read as a pair. The stepper becomes one control, not two.
The costLoses the instant across-the-room legibility of two different shapes, and takes vertical room above every selected character.

E · What ships today

CurrentFilled balloon & cloud

Your original mockup, taken literally: tinted fills, an offset shadow, a scalloped cloud. Legible, and it does read instantly — but it is the only thing on the canvas that isn't drawn, which is what makes it look pasted on.

Placement — a separate decision from finish

1 · Flanking (today)

Fixed offsets left and right of the node origin. Collides with the label, the link chips and long names.

2 · Fanned above

Both above the head, tilted apart from the crown. Anchored to the figure's actual top, so nothing below it is ever touched.

3 · Side-aware

Prefer flanking, but flip a bubble to the other side, or above, when its own side is crowded or off-canvas.

My recommendation: A (engraved plate) for the finish and 2 (fanned above) for the placement — the plate makes the bubbles the same material as everything else on the canvas, and fanning them above the crown removes the collision problem outright rather than nudging it around. If you want the map to have more character than that, C (banderole) is the braver choice and would be genuinely distinctive; it just needs a length cap. D is worth keeping in reserve: if the pair turns out to be read together far more often than separately, one card is the honest shape for it.
