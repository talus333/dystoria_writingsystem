# The Long Tally

## The Weir

Mirel Sarn came down to the water before the bell, because the tally was due and the tally did not wait for bells. The road out of Saltmarch was still dark. Frost had got into the ruts overnight and the mud held her boots at every step, and she thought, as she always thought on this walk, that a city built on a drowned field deserved the roads it got.

The Hollow Gate stood open. It always stood open; that was the joke of it. Two spans of black stone with no door between them, raised by people nobody remembered, and every cart and coffin and bride in Saltmarch had gone under it for four hundred years. Mirel touched the left pillar as she passed. Everyone did. The stone there was worn into a shallow dish the shape of a palm.

Old Halbrid had the ferry out already, poling along the near bank with a lantern hung from the bow. He raised a hand. Mirel raised one back.

"Tally day," he called.

"Tally day," she agreed.

"Vashet's man came through at second dark. Asked after your brother."

Mirel kept walking. Corvin was not her brother; he had married her sister and then buried her, and the word had stuck the way words do. She had stopped correcting people about it the year the weir broke. That was nine years ago now, and there were still mornings she came down this road expecting to see Hessa at the landing with her sleeves rolled.

The Tally Office sat where the Gullet narrowed, a long low building with too many doors, and Mirel had a key to three of them. Inside, the Long Tally lay open on its table the way it had lain open every day since the founding: eleven feet of hinged birchwood panels, every debt in Saltmarch cut into it in notches and saltglass beads. Nobody wrote in the Long Tally. You cut it, and the cut stayed.

She found her column. She found the notch. She stood looking at it for a while.

Corvin Ash owed the Communion of the Drowned four hundred marks against a boat that had never existed.

## Ilva's Landing

Corvin was not hard to find, which was his whole trouble. He was at the end of the quay at Ilva's Landing with his boots off and a saltglass knife in his good hand, scraping barnacles off a hull that belonged to somebody else. The scar on his left hand had gone white with the cold.

"You've been cut into the Tally," Mirel said.

"I know what I've been cut into."

"Four hundred marks, Corvin. Against a boat."

"Against a boat," he agreed, not looking up. "There's a boat. There'll be a boat. Ask me in the spring."

The Landing had been a shrine once, and then a fish market, and now it was mostly neither. The district took its name from Saint Ilva, who had walked out into the marsh in a bad year and not come back, and whom the Communion had been arguing about ever since — whether she had drowned or been taken, and what the difference was worth. Corvin had opinions on this. Corvin had opinions on most things that cost him nothing.

"The Marsh Watch has been asking," Mirel said.

That got his head up. "Dorne Vashet has been asking, you mean."

"The Prefect asks through the Watch. That's what a Watch is for."

"House Vashet has held that office three generations and I'd like somebody to tell me when they were elected to it." He wiped the knife on his knee. "What does she want?"

"You. Or the four hundred. She isn't fussy which."

He laughed at that, and then he stopped laughing, because they had both grown up in a city where the second thing was never really an option.

Down the quay, somebody's lantern went out. It was only a lantern; the wind came off the Gullet all winter and took them one at a time, and nobody looked up any more. But Mirel looked up, because she had been a tally-warden for eleven years and had learned that the thing you stop looking at is the thing that gets you.

## What the Drowned Are Owed

The Communion of the Drowned kept its house at the Ninth House, which had not been the ninth of anything for a long time and was not really a house. It was a hall, four storeys of grey brick with the water coming up through the cellar every tide, and the Communion had taken it in the year after the weir broke because nobody else wanted a building that filled twice a day.

Vessel Doret met her at the door. She was small and very old and had a way of standing that made doorways narrower.

"Warden."

"I'd like to see the entry."

"You've seen the Tally. The Tally is the entry."

"I'd like to see who cut it."

Doret considered her for long enough that Mirel understood the answer before it came. "The Communion does not cut the Tally," she said. "The Tally Office cuts the Tally. You cut the Tally, Warden Sarn. Perhaps you ought to ask yourself who was standing behind you when you did."

Mirel went back out past the Hollow Gate in the early dark and did not touch the pillar. She was thinking about the Breaking of the Weir, which she had been eleven years old for, and about how every account in Saltmarch had been re-cut in the six months after it, and about how nobody had ever asked who was holding the knife.

Tallywork is simple. That is what they tell you in your first week. A notch is a fact. A bead is a payment. The Tally does not lie, because the Tally cannot speak; it only keeps.

But a fact can be cut into a panel by anyone with a key to three doors, and Mirel Sarn had a key to three doors.

She stopped in the middle of the road with the frost coming up through her boots, and she understood, the way you understand a thing you have known for years without once looking at it, that the drowning-debt was not a debt at all. It was a way of moving people. It had always been a way of moving people. And she had spent eleven years keeping the book that did it.

She went home and did not sleep, and in the morning she went to the Tally Office before the bell, and she cut her own name out of the panel.
